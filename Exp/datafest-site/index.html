From 7 million health records to a mobile clinic route

At the 2026 ASA DataFest, my team was given more than 7 million rows of healthcare utilization, electronic health record, and social determinants of health data from Kansas and only 42 hours to turn them into a meaningful recommendation.

Rather than treating the challenge as a standard predictive modeling problem, I proposed that we build an end-to-end framework that answered three questions.

  • Who is most at risk? Patients with elevated social vulnerability who keep returning to the emergency department. See stops 1 and 2
  • Why are they using emergency care? Financial strain, and above all, getting to a clinic. See stop 3
  • What could realistically reduce it? A mobile clinic on a 15-stop route, tested in simulation. See stop 4
1

Diabetes sits at the center of the disease network

I led the modeling and analytical pipeline. We began by exploring disease relationships across the patient population using network analysis and Markov-chain-based transition patterns to understand how common chronic conditions co-occurred and progressed over time.

This analysis highlighted diabetes as a particularly important condition because of its strong connections with several high-burden comorbidities and its relationship with repeated healthcare utilization.

Social risk splits patients into three journeys

We also used K-means clustering to segment patients into groups with distinct clinical and utilization profiles. One cluster stood out: patients with elevated social vulnerability and repeated dependence on emergency departments, even when many of their conditions could potentially have been managed through primary or preventive care.

  • Cluster 1: DisengagedLongest gap between visits, lowest ED rate, fewest visits.
  • Cluster 2: Complex frequentShortest gap, high diagnosis count, most irregular rhythm.
  • Cluster 3: ED-dependentHighest ED rate, moderate visit volume. Social barriers delay care.
3

The data pointed at access, not at a better model

I then built a logistic regression model to quantify which factors were most strongly associated with avoidable emergency department visits. The model showed that financial barriers were important, but transportation access also emerged as one of the strongest predictors.

That result became the turning point in our project. Instead of spending the remaining time tuning models for marginal improvements in predictive performance, we shifted our focus toward designing an intervention that directly addressed the access problem identified by the data.

A mobile clinic, routed to where patients are

We proposed a mobile healthcare clinic targeted toward underserved and high-risk patient populations.

Turning intermediate outputs into one story

Throughout the competition, I also coordinated closely with teammates working on data cleaning and visualization. Because the dataset was large and the timeline was extremely short, one of the biggest challenges was making sure that each technical component contributed to a coherent final story.

I regularly translated intermediate model outputs into interpretable findings so the team could decide quickly which analyses were worth pursuing and which were not.

What stayed with me

The strongest data science project is not necessarily the one with the most complicated model.

The value came from connecting statistical evidence to a concrete decision: moving from millions of messy records, to identifying a vulnerable population, to understanding the mechanism behind their healthcare use, and finally to designing and evaluating an intervention that a policymaker could realistically consider implementing.
