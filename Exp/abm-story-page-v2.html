// research, tsinghua university

I rebuilt an economy from imitating households to see if booms, busts, and inequality appear on their own.

The question
Can business cycles and wealth inequality emerge with no external shocks and no perfectly rational decision-makers?
What I built
A simulated economy of many households that each copy the savings rate of their highest-consuming neighbor, later extended with LLM agents.
What I found
Copy too often and the economy falls into a poverty trap. Copy less often and cycles and inequality appear on their own.

// 01 the classic model

The textbook economy has one perfectly rational household

The Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans growth model starts with a single representative household that optimizes its lifetime utility. In that framework, business cycles have to come from outside shocks.

Real households miss the optimal path by 30 to 50%

// 02 the swap

I replaced it with a crowd

Instead of one optimizer, many heterogeneous households. Each keeps its own capital, income, consumption, and savings rate, and output follows a Cobb–Douglas production structure. Nobody solves an optimization problem.

// 03 the network

They can only see their neighbors

Households sit on a social network (an Erdős–Rényi random graph). Each one observes only the households it is connected to.

// 04 the rule

One simple rule replaces optimization

At random, Poisson-distributed times, a household finds the neighbor with the highest current consumption and adopts that neighbor's savings rate, plus about ±1% of exploration noise. That is the whole decision rule.

Try it yourself

// 05 copy too often

Copy too often and the economy stalls

Frequent copying reacts to short-term differences in consumption. High consumption often means low saving, so low-saving habits spread. Savings collapse, capital stops accumulating, and everyone ends up equally poor: a poverty trap.

// 06 copy less often

Copy less often and cycles and inequality appear

When households keep their strategy longer, differences in capital have time to build. Output starts oscillating on its own, and capital splits into a high-wealth group and a low-wealth group. No shocks were added.

Why does it cycle?

Recovery: poor households copy high-saving capitalists and start accumulating capital, so the economy expands. Peak: the return on capital falls, and rich households now see poor households with low savings but high short-run consumption and mistakenly copy them, depleting capital. The economy contracts.

// 07 language models

Then I let language models make the decisions

Only after validating the rule-based baseline did I swap the rule for LLM decision modules. Each agent receives its own capital, income, neighbor states, and aggregate indicators, and must reply with a structured JSON action, so every decision can be validated and compared with the baseline.

// 08 echo chambers

Agents who mostly talk to people like them split into camps

With strong homophily, where agents interact mostly with others who already agree, the belief distribution shifted from one cluster to separated clusters. I measured it with variance, distribution shape, and the separation between clusters.

// under the hood

Six steps, always in the same order

Before adding any LLM, I built a deterministic rule-based baseline so the economic mechanism itself could be understood and validated. Every simulation tick runs in a strict order, because changing when agents observe or act can change the feedback structure of the whole system.

  1. EnvironmentCompute total capital, output, the return on capital, and the wage.
  2. ObserveEach household reads its neighbors' states.
  3. DecideApply the imitation rule where a Poisson update is due.
  4. ExecuteUpdate the savings rates.
  5. AggregateRecompute output, capital, the Gini coefficient, and more.
  6. LogRecord the tick to MLflow.

Households update at Poisson-distributed times with mean interval τ, on an Erdős–Rényi network. The update interval τ maps onto the classical discount rate: short intervals behave like an extremely short-sighted household, and long intervals approach a fully patient one.

// engineering

Making a random system repeatable

Agent-based systems are already random, and LLM-generated behavior adds another source of variation. So the experiments were built to be repeatable, and to separate robust patterns from one-configuration accidents.

  • Fixed random seeds wherever possible.
  • Versioned configurations, stored with the experiment logs.
  • MLflow tracking of runs, network structures, model parameters, and metric time series.
  • Sensitivity analysis across imitation strength, exploration noise, update frequency, and network connectivity.
  • Baseline first: the deterministic model was validated before any stochastic or LLM component.

// takeaways

What I took away

Simple agents can produce complex economies

Complex macro-level behavior does not necessarily require complex individual agents. Simple local rules can generate feedback, inequality, and persistent cycles.

Look at the crowd, not the single agent

The interesting question about LLM agents is what happens when many of them repeatedly interact. Small differences in rules, networks, timing, or information can compound.

Build the interpretable baseline first

Because I validated a transparent baseline before adding language models, I could compare complex agents against a benchmark instead of treating the model as the explanation.

What the model suggests for policy

Update frequency matters

Moderating the speed of social financial imitation could help economies avoid poverty traps.

Network density matters

Denser, better-connected networks are more stable and less prone to inequality-generating phase transitions.

A small rational anchor is enough

A 5% share of agents holding long-run savings targets shortens and regularizes cycles.

It became one of my first experiences thinking about AI not just as a predictive model, but as a collection of autonomous decision-makers whose interactions can produce unexpected behavior at the system level.
