// directed reading, spring 2026

I used nonlinear dynamics to explain when a tumour escapes immune control.

the question
Can qualitative changes in a system (its equilibria, bifurcations, limit cycles, and basins of attraction) explain transitions between immune control, dormancy, oscillatory relapse, and escape?
what I built
A two-equation model of T cells and tumour cells, analysed with equilibria, Jacobians, phase portraits, and bifurcations, then extended with space, delay, noise, and optimal control.
what I found
Elimination, dormancy, oscillation, and escape are different dynamical regimes separated by thresholds, so therapy should move a patient into a different basin, not just shrink the tumour.

// 01 the model

Two populations, one feedback loop

Cytotoxic T cells c(t) and tumour cells n(t) interact. Tumour cells recruit immune cells, immune cells kill tumour cells, and their effectiveness fades through exhaustion and death while the tumour keeps growing toward its carrying capacity. Long-term behaviour depends on the balance, not on either population alone.

R₂ = γₙS / (μcαₙ) > 1 immune control, < 1 tumour invades

Immune equation: baseline influx, saturating recruitment, exhaustion, natural death. Tumour equation: logistic growth minus immune killing. Checkpoint inhibitors lower γc, which raises R₂.

// 02 equilibria

Fixed points are clinical states

Where neither population changes, the system sits at an equilibrium: tumour elimination, persistent coexistence (dormancy), or tumour-dominated escape. Evaluating the Jacobian at each point, its trace, determinant, and eigenvalues classify it as a stable node, stable spiral, unstable spiral, saddle, or Hopf point.

Here, at this setting, there are two stable states (dormancy and escape) separated by a saddle.

// 03 basins

Same parameters, different outcomes

A saddle's stable manifold acts as a separatrix. Start on one side and the system drifts to dormancy, start on the other and it escapes. Outcome depends on the initial condition (c₀, n₀), not only on dose or a single parameter.

// 04 the knob

Turn one parameter and the structure changes

Instead of asking only whether a point is stable at one setting, I tracked how behaviour changes as the immune-killing rate γₙ varies. Equilibria appear, merge, and vanish. Watch the sweep: it crosses an invasion threshold, then creates and destroys pairs of equilibria, which gives bistability and hysteresis.

Clinically, a small reversal in a parameter does not necessarily return the patient to the previous state, because the system may already sit in a different basin.

// 05 four regimes

Elimination, dormancy, oscillation, escape

In my analysis the killing rate passes four thresholds as it decreases, and each marks a qualitatively different change:

  1. Transcritical at R₂ = 1: the invasion threshold, where stability passes between equilibria.
  2. Saddle-node: pairs of equilibria are created or destroyed, giving bistability and hysteresis.
  3. Hopf: complex eigenvalues cross the imaginary axis and the system oscillates, a mathematical analogue of periodic relapse and regression.
  4. Homoclinic: the cycle meets a saddle and the system moves toward escape.

The parameter sets behind my slide figures are not listed, so the live model uses illustrative values inside the slides' ranges. It shows elimination, dormancy, bistability, and escape, but not a sustained oscillation.

// takeaways

Nonlinearity creates structure

Therapy should move the system to a different dynamical basin, not just reduce tumour size.

A tumour does not necessarily move smoothly from "better" to "worse". A system can look stable for a long time and then cross a threshold after which its behaviour changes qualitatively. A patient's prognosis can change because the geometry of the system changed, not simply because the tumour grew by a fixed percentage.

What I am most proud of

The project moved beyond simply solving differential equations. I used the mathematics to ask a more applied question: what does a change in stability, a separatrix, or a bifurcation actually mean for tumour progression and treatment? That perspective, using mathematical structure to understand when and why clinical regimes change, is what made it interesting to me.
