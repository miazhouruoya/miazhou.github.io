Neural population decoding and thalamocortical timing in a visual decision task

What can a mouse's brain activity tell us before it decides?

Question
When does information appear in different brain regions, and what can be decoded from population activity before a decision is made?
Approach
Analyzed International Brain Laboratory recordings from mice doing a visual decision task. I treated the recordings as population signals: timing between thalamus (LGd) and cortex (VISa), then classifiers for prior expectation, stimulus, and choice.
Bottom line
Activity before the stimulus predicts the experimental block bias, and that result is significant. The timing lead and the zero-contrast choice effect are consistent with our hypotheses but did not reach significance.

Task and data

Setup

Mice responded to visual stimuli by turning a wheel while neural activity was recorded across multiple brain regions. The recordings include sensory, behavioral, and reaction-time variables, so neural responses can be tied to both stimulus processing and choice.

Subjects
115 mice (80 males, 35 females)
Task
Visuomotor decision-making with a rotating wheel
Variables
Visual stimulus, choice, feedback (reward or noise), reaction time
Goal
Brain-wide neural correlates of sensory, motor, and cognitive variables
Blocks

The task has blocks with different probabilities that the stimulus appears on the left. Over time the mouse can learn the block and use it as a prior expectation.

02

Timing: LGd and VISa

Question

Does the thalamic relay LGd respond before the cortical area VISa, as thalamocortical information flow would predict?

Method

Estimated the peak-response latency of each population after stimulus onset, compared latency distributions across four recording sessions, and used cross-correlation to measure the relative timing of the two signals.

Result
  • LGd responded earlier than VISa in all four sessions, and VISa's latencies were more variable (Figure 1).
  • Cross-correlation put LGd ahead of VISa by about 56 ms on average (Figure 2).
  • Across sessions this was not significant (p = 0.1811), and several individual sessions were inconsistent.
How far this goes

Consistent with a possible thalamocortical timing hierarchy, but underpowered. It is not evidence of causal information flow, and it needs more sessions.

The single-session first look

Early analyses of single sessions gave different absolute latencies, which shows how noisy neural timing is and how much it depends on which neurons are sampled.

03

Prior expectations

Question

Does activity before the stimulus contain the mouse's expectation, and does that expectation relate to its choice?

Method

Used pre-stimulus activity, from about −0.5 to 0 s before stimulus onset, to decode (a) the block bias and (b) the left or right choice. For choice, I compared all trials with zero-contrast trials, where there is effectively no informative stimulus and expectations should matter more.

How far this goes

The block-bias result is significant. The choice result is preliminary: the comparisons were not significant and come from a single session, so it needs region- and session-level validation before it can be generalized.

04

Decoding across regions

Question

Which regions carry information about the stimulus, and which about the decision?

Method

Compared ensemble, tree-based, and linear classifiers (Random Forest, gradient boosting, SVM, logistic regression, linear discriminant analysis), with and without PCA, using accuracy, AUC, recall, precision, F1, Cohen's kappa, and MCC. The goal was to see where different information lives, not just to maximize accuracy.

Inside the population

Neural recordings hold hundreds or thousands of correlated features, so PCA compressed activity into lower-dimensional representations that keep the dominant variance. PSTHs split by choice show when left and right responses diverge, and feature importance shows which neurons and regions carry the most weight: a few VISpm neurons lead, followed by APN neurons.

05

Takeaways

  1. A pattern is not evidence.A visually convincing difference between two regions does not automatically mean a robust mechanism. We kept separating descriptive patterns from statistical evidence.
  2. Decoding is not causation.If a classifier predicts choice from a region, the region's activity contains choice-related information. It does not mean the region caused the decision.
  3. Sampling changes results.A model can score well in one session and fail in another purely because the recorded neurons differ, so an apparent 30–50 ms lead changes meaning once session variability and sample size are considered.
What stayed with me

When visual evidence disappeared, the pre-stimulus neural state became comparatively more useful for predicting behavior. That gave me a way to think about decision-making as an interaction between incoming sensory information and an internal prior, rather than a simple stimulus-to-response pipeline.

What I am most proud of

Our project connected several levels of analysis into one workflow and kept tying the computational results back to a biological hypothesis about how thalamic and cortical regions process visual information and how internal expectations influence decisions. It taught me that the harder problem is not building an accurate decoder but working out what it tells you, and being disciplined about separating a promising pattern from a conclusion the data cannot yet support.
